Lab 13 · threat intelligence · the human layer

The Lantern Rotation.

The classic kill chain tracks an actor. But a six-month intrusion is not one person — it's a shift rotation. The operator tires; his tooling becomes known even to his own team; his position goes dark. So he passes the access shell to a teammate and steps out of the vigil, and a fresh hand picks up the lantern and carries the campaign on. The access persists; the human is the variable. And the human has a pulse — working hours, habits, discipline, tooling — that changes when the hand changes. That change is a seam, and a seam is something you can see.

Analysis only. This models the adversary's human factors so a defender can detect and reason about them. It is not a playbook for conducting operations and teaches no operator tradecraft — only how operator behaviour leaks into your telemetry.
01 — the psychological kill chain

The operator's arc, and what each stage leaks

Overlay a human lifecycle on the intrusion. Each state of the operator leaves a different tell in the logs — and the last one, the rotation, is the loudest.

1
Fresh entry
A new hand on the keyboard is careful and slow, minimizing noise while learning unfamiliar terrain.
tell: low error rate, heavy reconnaissance of the environment
2
Establishment
Persistence and C2 go in; the operator settles into a toolset.
tell: a stable tooling / C2 fingerprint forms
3
Routine
Steady cadence, consistent working hours — a human rhythm you can baseline.
tell: a predictable active-hours window (their timezone)
4
Fatigue
Boredom and overfamiliarity breed shortcuts; discipline slips.
tell: OPSEC error rate climbs, tooling reused carelessly
5
Exposure
The tradecraft is now known — to defenders, and to the operator's own team, which is the cue to rotate.
tell: over-used indicators, signatures catching up
6
Handoff — the Lantern Rotation
The access shell passes to a fresh operator. A new human inherits the terrain and starts the arc over.
tell: a discontinuity — working hours jump (new timezone), OPSEC snaps back to careful, the “known” environment gets re-reconned, tooling swaps

03 — the payoff & the hard part

Your “actor” is a relay

The reason this matters isn't trivia — it corrects a mistake defenders make constantly: collapsing a long campaign into one persona.

  • Attribution honesty. A six-month “APT29 operator” in your timeline is almost certainly several humans. Treating them as one averages away the very discontinuities that are your best evidence — and your best detection windows. This sharpens Lab 7: an actor vertex is a team, not a person.
  • The seam is where mistakes live. A fresh operator re-learns the environment and makes the errors a veteran wouldn't. The handoff is the single best moment to catch the campaign — if you're watching for the discontinuity instead of smoothing over it.
  • Drift is not always a handoff. The same operator learns the network, automates, and shifts hours too. Benign drift is gradual; a rotation is a step. One discontinuity is a lead, not a conviction.
  • Hands can be hidden. A disciplined team standardizes tooling and hours to erase the seam, and timezones can be spoofed. Behavioural biometrics are noisy and ethically fraught. You weight this signal; you never rest a case on it alone.
